\begin{abstract}
Timbre morphing, the generation of intermediate timbres between known sounds,
expands the sonic palette for music production and sound design. Common
techniques interpolate in the latent space of a single generative model trained
on multiple timbres, which in small models compromises the fidelity of each one.
We propose an alternative: training variational autoencoders ($\beta$-VAE)
specialized per timbre, and generating hybrid timbres by interpolating directly
in the model weight space. The system is trained on STFT magnitude spectrograms
from four sources (piano, guitar, voice and bass) and evaluated with Fr\'echet
Audio Distance over MERT embeddings. A $2\times2$ ablation shows that
fine-tuning every model from a common base model, rather than training it from
scratch, makes the transfer towards the target monotonic, while latent-space
regularization keeps unrelated timbres unaffected; only their combination yields
gradual and predictable transitions. The models are small, and the
weight-interpolated decoder runs in real time in a web browser.
\end{abstract}
%
\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Timbre is the perceptual attribute that lets us distinguish two sounds of equal
pitch and loudness produced by different sources. Generating intermediate
timbres between two known sounds---timbre \emph{morphing}---is of particular
interest for music production and sound design, as it enables hybrid sonorities
that belong to no single source.

The most widespread deep-learning approaches learn a single generative model
over multiple timbres and interpolate within its latent space; however, when the
model is small this strategy tends to compromise the fidelity of each
individual timbre (in our setting, per-instrument models reconstruct better than
a shared model of the same size; Sec.~\ref{sec:arch}). This work explores an
alternative: training a specialized model per timbre and interpolating directly
in the space of their \emph{weights}, so as to preserve the reconstruction
quality of each source model. A design principle throughout is to keep the
models deliberately small, so that both inference and weight interpolation run
on modest hardware and in real time, aiming at a practical tool for performance
and production.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.} (i) We propose a timbre-morphing technique that
interpolates the \emph{weights} of per-instrument specialized VAEs, rather than
the latent code of a single shared model, and evaluate it on four sound sources.
(ii) Through a $2\times2$ ablation over initialization (shared checkpoint vs.\
scratch) and latent regularization ($\beta$-VAE vs.\ AE), we provide initial
evidence that both factors contribute to well-behaved interpolations, each to a
different aspect. (iii) We show that the weight-interpolated decoder runs in
real time in a web browser, and release code, models, audio examples and an
interactive demo.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/facundo2gonzalez2/2ASMRS}}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

Audio morphing can be organized by \emph{where} the interpolation takes place:
on the \textbf{signal}, on the \textbf{latent space} of a generative model,
or---as we propose---on the model \textbf{weights}. A foundational signal-domain
method \cite{slaney1996audiomorphing} aligns two spectrograms with dynamic time
warping and cross-fades them; it is training-free but requires explicit
alignment and was validated only on voice. With deep learning, morphing moved to
the latent space. NSynth \cite{engel2017nsynth} interpolates note embeddings in a
\emph{single} WaveNet-decoder autoencoder trained on all instruments, and Magenta
turned it into an interactive grid \cite{magenta2017nsynthsoundmaker} close to the
tool motivating our work. Its autoregressive decoder, however, precludes
real-time synthesis.

Recent work relies on latent diffusion, with high quality at high cost. Latent
diffusion bridges \cite{mancusi2025latentbridges} train one diffusion model per
instrument connected by a shared prior, but perform full timbre \emph{transfer}
rather than controllable intermediate states. SoundMorpher
\cite{niu2024soundmorpher} morphs in the noise and embedding space of a
pretrained text-to-audio model and introduces perceptually uniform morphing, at
the cost of a large model. For guitar tones, Chen \emph{et al.}
\cite{chen2025guitartonemorphing} find that a lightweight autoencoder beats
fine-tuned diffusion, and one variant interpolates network weights, though
restricted to a single instrument. Mix2Morph \cite{chu2026mix2morph} trains a
large text-to-audio model to morph from noisy mixes. Overall, the literature
interpolates the \emph{content} inside a fixed model.

Interpolating network \emph{weights} has been studied mainly outside audio.
Averaging models fine-tuned from a shared pre-trained checkpoint improves
accuracy at no inference cost \cite{wortsman2022soups}, and weight differences
act as composable ``task vectors'' \cite{ilharco2023task}. This works because
models trained from a common starting point stay in the same loss basin and are
linearly connected \cite{frankle2020lmc,neyshabur2020transfer}, which motivates
our \emph{Checkpoint} strategy. Closest to our setting, Murata \emph{et al.}
\cite{murata2024attribute} merge text-to-speech models to interpolate speaker and
emotion attributes. Spherical interpolation \cite{shoemake1985slerp} is common
for latent vectors of generative models \cite{white2016sampling}; we apply it
per parameter tensor.

Variational autoencoders (VAE) \cite{kingma2013auto} impose a probabilistic
structure on the latent space via the Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence, which
facilitates sampling and interpolation; the $\beta$-VAE \cite{higgins2017beta}
weights this term by $\beta$. For music sound modeling, variational models offer
the best fidelity/regularization trade-off
\cite{roche2019autoencodersmusicsoundmodeling}; RAVE \cite{caillon2021rave}
showed real-time VAE synthesis; and sparse autoencoders build perceptually
consistent timbre spaces \cite{riera2017timbre}.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}
We use four instruments with clearly distinguishable timbres: piano, guitar,
bass and voice, with about 87 minutes of audio each (5.8 hours in
total, balanced to avoid biasing training). Piano comes from 20 freely
licensed tracks: Mozart performances by M.~Staab and B.~Kinsella (via Chosic),
royalty-free tracks from Pixabay, and nine AI-generated solo-piano samples from
ElevenLabs (per-track sources and licenses are listed in the repository); guitar from a subset of GuitarSet \cite{xi2019guitarset}
(electric guitar, melodic and chordal passages); bass from jazz-standard
basslines in FiloBass \cite{riley2023filobass}; and voice from a subset of
VocalSet \cite{wilkins2018vocalset} (two male and two female voices, with
melodies, scales and arpeggios).

All tracks are converted to mono at $22050$~Hz and analyzed with an STFT
($2048$-sample Hann window, $512$-sample hop). The model works only with the
magnitude, in decibels $S = 10\log_{10}(|F|^2)$; values below $-60$~dB are
clipped, the floor is shifted to zero and the result is divided by the global
maximum $X_{max}$ (stored with the model), mapping magnitudes to $[0,1]$. Each
$1025$-bin frame is an independent input to the network.

\subsection{Variational Autoencoder and Architecture}
The encoder approximates the posterior $q(z\mid x)$ and the decoder the
likelihood $p(x\mid z)$, with prior $p(z)=\mathcal{N}(0,I)$. The $\beta$-VAE
\cite{higgins2017beta} minimizes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}} + \beta\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}$ is the mean squared error (MSE) between input
and reconstruction and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}$ the closed-form KL divergence
to the prior; $z$ is sampled with the reparameterization trick. When $\beta=0$
the model reduces to a deterministic autoencoder (AE) and we take $z=\mu$.
Higher $\beta$ yields a smoother latent space at the cost of fidelity.

The encoder is a stack of fully connected layers
$1025\!\rightarrow\!(1024,512,256,128,64,32,16,8)\!\rightarrow\!4$, each hidden
layer combining a linear map, Batch Normalization (BN) and an ELU activation,
with two heads for $\mu$ and $\log\sigma^2$. The decoder mirrors it back to
$1025$ bins, with a final linear layer and BN but no activation. The full model
has $3.5$\,M parameters, of which $1.76$\,M belong to the decoder ($\approx3.5$
MFLOP per frame).

\subsection{Training Strategy}
We optimize Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss} with Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$), a $95/5$
train/validation split, batches of $256$ frames, early stopping on the
validation loss and gradient clipping. Per-instrument models follow two
initialization strategies. \emph{Scratch}: each model is trained from a random
initialization on its instrument only. \emph{Checkpoint}: we first train a
\emph{base model} on all four instruments and then fine-tune one model per
instrument from that common starting point. Crossing both initializations with
two regularization settings---AE ($\beta=0$) and $\beta$-VAE
($\beta=0.001$)---gives a $2\times2$ ablation whose baseline, Scratch-AE, uses
neither factor.

\subsection{Weight Interpolation}
Given two models with identical architecture, a source A and a target B, and
$\alpha\in[0,1]$, we build an intermediate model by interpolating \emph{every}
parameter tensor of A and B (encoder and decoder) independently; synthesis then
uses only the resulting decoder. By construction $\alpha=0$ reproduces A and
$\alpha=1$ reproduces B. We consider linear interpolation (LERP),
$(1-\alpha)\,w_A + \alpha\,w_B$, and spherical interpolation (SLERP), which
traverses the arc joining both flattened tensors:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:slerp}
\mathrm{SLERP}(w_A,w_B,\alpha)=
\frac{\sin\!\big((1-\alpha)\theta\big)}{\sin\theta}\,w_A +
\frac{\sin\!\big(\alpha\theta\big)}{\sin\theta}\,w_B,
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ is the angle between $w_A$ and $w_B$, computed per tensor. When
$|\cos\theta|>0.9995$, SLERP is numerically unstable and we fall back to LERP.
The BN running means and variances are floating-point and are interpolated like
any other tensor. Only the integer BN step counter, which differs between models
and is unused at inference, takes the value of the closer model.

\subsection{Audio Reconstruction and Metrics}
The decoder output is rescaled by $X_{max}$, converted to linear magnitude and
inverted with a phase estimate: Griffin--Lim, PGHI \cite{prusa2017pghi} or random
phase. For evaluation we use (i) validation MSE, and (ii) a Fr\'echet Audio
Distance (FAD) \cite{gui2024adaptingfrechetaudiodistance} over embeddings from
MERT-v1-95M \cite{li2023mert}, mapped to a bounded similarity
$\mathrm{sim}=\exp(-\mathrm{FAD}/k)$ with $k=585$ (a monotone rescaling that does
not alter any comparison). FAD is computed at the \emph{clip level}, between the
distributions of the 768-d MERT frame embeddings (75 frames/s) of two individual
clips. In the interpolation experiments each clip has 48 STFT frames
($\approx1.1$~s, $\approx83$ MERT frames), so the covariances are rank-deficient
and are regularized with $\varepsilon I$ ($\varepsilon\le10^{-1}$). Clip-level
FAD is known to be biased at small sample sizes \cite{chung2025kad}, so we first
check its behaviour on a controlled case (Sec.~\ref{sec:metric}).

\section{Experiments and Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Architecture and $\beta$ Selection}\label{sec:arch}
Sweeping latent dimensions $\{2,3,4,6,8\}$, a dimension of $4$ gives a
significant reduction of validation error over $2$ and $3$, while $6$ or $8$ add
little, consistently for every per-instrument model, for the base model and for
the KL term; since four control knobs are far more manipulable than eight, we
adopt $4$. Sweeping $\beta$ on the base model, the validation MSE ($\times10^{-3}$)
and KL are $7.71/0.30$ for $\beta=10^{-2}$, $3.50/1.50$ for $10^{-3}$, $2.88/2.85$
for $10^{-4}$, $2.28/4.93$ for $10^{-5}$ and $2.27/386$ for $\beta=0$.
Regularizing more than $10^{-3}$ doubles the error, while relaxing it lowers the
error by $18$--$35\%$ but doubles or triples the KL; we adopt $\beta=0.001$ as
the compromise that favours a regular latent space, the property interpolation
relies on. With the same architecture, the specialized models reconstruct their
instrument better than the shared base model: mean MSE $2.06$ (both AE
configurations) vs.\ $2.28$ for $\beta=0$, and $3.34$ (Checkpoint) vs.\ $3.61$ for
$\beta=0.001$; per-configuration
values are in Table~\ref{tab:results}. Each model is validated on its own
split, so this comparison is indicative.

\subsection{Latent-Space Analysis}

Projecting the base-model latent embeddings to 2-D with UMAP ($n\_neighbors=15$),
the four instruments form clearly separated clusters under both $\beta=0$ and
$\beta=0.001$, with pairwise multidimensional Wilcoxon--Mann--Whitney $U$
statistics close to $1$ ($0.5$ = indistinguishable, $1$ = fully separated).
Separation is consistently slightly lower with $\beta=0.001$, as expected from a
smoother latent space (e.g.\ $0.926\rightarrow0.796$ for bass--voice;
Fig.~\ref{fig:umap}).

\subsection{Metric Sanity Check}\label{sec:metric}
Before evaluating the models, we check the metric on a case whose outcome is
known in advance and that involves no model: a waveform cross-fade
$(1-\alpha)A + \alpha B$ between original clips of two instruments
(piano$\rightarrow$guitar and voice$\rightarrow$piano, 20 random 1.5~s clip pairs
each), compared against $A$, $B$ and a clip of a third, \emph{witness},
instrument. Similarity to $A$ decreases monotonically from $1$ to $0.82$, similarity
to $B$ mirrors it, the curves cross at $\alpha\approx0.5$, and similarity to the
witness stays flat ($0.82\pm0.02$). Two clips of different instruments thus score
$\approx0.82$ (FAD $\approx116$), which sets the useful range of the metric in our
setting; the values in Fig.~\ref{fig:cross} are similarities, not raw FAD.
Cosine similarity between mean MERT embeddings follows the same trends but is
more dispersed on time-varying latent inputs, so we use FAD in what follows.

\subsection{Weight-Interpolation Experiments}
Averaged over every source/target instrument pair, the similarity to the target
grows as $\alpha$ increases in all four configurations. Checkpoint-$\beta$-VAE
shows the smoothest growth and Scratch-AE the worst, delaying the change to
extreme values of $\alpha$.

A stricter test is the cross-interpolation property: as the output approaches B
it must move away from A while \emph{not} acquiring traits of a witness
instrument that takes no part in the interpolation. Each of 25 latent vectors
$z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ is held constant over a clip and decoded with random
phase by the interpolated model and, as references, by the pure A, B and witness
models. Fig.~\ref{fig:cross} shows piano$\rightarrow$guitar with voice as witness,
and Table~\ref{tab:results} summarizes this pair and piano$\rightarrow$voice
(guitar as witness). Similarity at $\alpha=0$ peaks at $\approx0.91$ rather than
$1$ because each random-phase rendering differs.

The two factors of the ablation affect different aspects of the transition.
\emph{Initialization} determines whether the transfer towards B is monotonic:
$\rho_B$ is $0.84$--$0.95$ for Checkpoint models but only $0.36$--$0.50$ for
Scratch models, whose similarity to B barely grows until $\alpha\approx0.9$. \emph{Regularization} matters within the Checkpoint
models. Without it the transition happens abruptly (crossover at
$\alpha\approx0.27$) and the witness similarity drifts three times more
($\Delta_W=0.08$--$0.10$ vs.\ $0.03$--$0.04$), meaning the intermediate decoders
visit timbres unrelated to either endpoint. Only Checkpoint-$\beta$-VAE combines
a monotonic transfer, a centered crossover ($\alpha_\times=0.55$--$0.59$) and a
flat witness, consistent with linear connectivity of models fine-tuned from a
common checkpoint \cite{frankle2020lmc,neyshabur2020transfer}. Audio examples of
continuous transitions for the best and worst configurations, and of
audio-to-audio morphing between real recordings, are linked in the repository.

\subsection{Real-Time Operation}
We implemented the interpolated decoder in a browser synthesizer (ONNX Runtime
Web, single-threaded WebAssembly), measured in Firefox on a laptop CPU (Intel
Core i7-1255U). SLERP angles depend only on the weights and are
precomputed once per model pair. Each frame then evaluates both endpoint decoders
($\approx7$~MFLOP) and takes $\approx1$--$2$~ms, and real-time phase reconstruction
(RTPGHI) takes $0.6$~ms, against a $23.2$~ms hop budget. The resulting
end-to-end latency is $\approx70$~ms, while $\alpha$ and $z$ are modulated
continuously.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

We presented a technique to synthesize intermediate timbres by interpolating the
weights of per-instrument specialized VAEs, instead of interpolating the latent
space of a single model trained on all instruments. A $2\times2$ ablation shows
that interpolation quality depends strongly on the training configuration.
Fine-tuning from a common base model makes the transfer monotonic, and
latent-space regularization keeps it gradual and free of unrelated timbres.
Their combination (Checkpoint-$\beta$-VAE) produced the most predictable
transitions. The models are small enough for the interpolated decoder to run in
real time in a web browser.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations.} The evaluation covers four sources and relies on a
clip-level FAD over a single embedding model; no formal listening test was
conducted, and similarity to the endpoints cannot distinguish a superposition of
two sources from a fused timbre. We did not compare against large latent-space
morphing systems, whose scale and control paradigm differ. Future work includes
a perceptual study, KAD \cite{chung2025kad} and alternative embeddings, a
latent-interpolation baseline on the shared base model, more instruments, and a
triple interpolation controlled by two parameters.
